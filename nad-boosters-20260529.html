1. Introduction: Why NAD+ Matters for Longevity

Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide â€” NAD+ for short â€” is one of the most important molecules you've never heard of. It's a coenzyme found in every cell in your body, and it's essential for life. Without NAD+, you'd be dead in seconds.

NAD+ powers hundreds of cellular reactions. It helps convert food into energy. It repairs damaged DNA. It regulates your circadian rhythm. And it activates sirtuins â€” a family of proteins that scientists call 'longevity genes' because of their role in cellular repair and stress resistance.

Here's the problem: NAD+ levels decline dramatically with age. By age 50, your NAD+ levels have dropped by roughly 50%. By age 80, they may be just 10-20% of what they were in your youth. This decline isn't just a biomarker â€” it's believed to be a driver of aging itself. Low NAD+ means less DNA repair, less mitochondrial function, less cellular maintenance. The result: accelerated aging and increased disease risk.

This discovery has spawned a multi-hundred-million-dollar industry. Since 2018, companies have sold NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide) and NR (nicotinamide riboside) supplements promising to restore youthful NAD+ levels. Early studies in mice were stunning â€” NR extended lifespan, improved metabolic health, and enhanced cognitive function. The human trials, however, have been more modest. And in 2022, the FDA threw a wrench in the works by declaring NMN an investigational new drug, effectively banning it from being sold as a supplement in the United States.

So what's the real story? Do NAD+ boosters work in humans? Is NMN better than NR? What about plain old niacin (vitamin B3), which costs pennies per day? And what are the risks?

This article breaks down the science, the evidence, and the practical considerations for anyone considering NAD+ supplementation.

2. The Science: How NAD+ Works in Your Cells

What Is NAD+?

NAD+ exists in two forms: NAD+ (oxidized) and NADH (reduced). This redox cycling allows it to transfer electrons during metabolic reactions â€” essentially shuttling energy from food to your mitochondria, the power plants of your cells.

But NAD+ does more than just energy transfer. In its oxidized form (NAD+), it acts as a signaling molecule. When DNA is damaged, PARP enzymes consume NAD+ to repair it. When you're fasting or exercising, NAD+ levels rise, activating sirtuins that trigger protective responses. When you overeat or get too little sleep, NAD+ levels fall, impairing these protective mechanisms.

The Sirtuin Connection

Sirtuins are a family of seven proteins (SIRT1-7) that regulate everything from DNA repair to mitochondrial biogenesis to inflammation. They were first discovered in yeast, where the sirtuin gene SIR2 extended lifespan by 30%. In mammals, sirtuins require NAD+ to function â€” they literally can't do their job without it.

Think of sirtuins as cellular maintenance workers. When NAD+ is abundant, they go to work: repairing DNA, cleaning up damaged mitochondria, reducing inflammation, and coordinating stress responses. When NAD+ is scarce, they sit idle, and cellular damage accumulates.

Why NAD+ Declines With Age

Three main factors drive NAD+ decline:

  1. Increased consumption: As we age, DNA damage accumulates, forcing PARP enzymes to consume more NAD+ for repairs. Chronic inflammation also increases NAD+ consumption by immune cells.

  2. Decreased production: The enzymes that synthesize NAD+ (particularly NAMPT) become less efficient with age. It's like a factory slowing down while demand increases.

  3. CD38 enzyme: This enzyme on immune cells consumes NAD+ at an accelerating rate as we age. Research shows CD38 activity increases with age and may be the single biggest driver of NAD+ decline.

3. Human Evidence: What the Clinical Trials Show

Nicotinamide Riboside (NR)

NR has the longest track record in human trials. It's a form of vitamin B3 that's one step closer to NAD+ than niacin, requiring fewer enzymatic conversions.

Key trials:

  • Trammell et al. (2016): First human study showing NR safely raises blood NAD+ levels. Single doses of 100-1000mg were well-tolerated.

  • Martens et al. (2018): In healthy older adults (ages 55-79), 1000mg/day NR for 6 weeks increased NAD+ by 60% and improved blood pressure (diastolic reduced by 7 points) and arterial stiffness.

  • Dollerup et al. (2020): In obese men, 2000mg/day NR for 12 weeks increased NAD+ but did NOT improve insulin sensitivity or mitochondrial function â€” a sobering result.

  • Bieganowski & Brenner studies: Showed NR is orally bioavailable and efficiently converted to NAD+ in humans.

Bottom line on NR: It reliably raises NAD+ levels, but the health benefits in humans have been modest compared to mouse studies. The blood pressure improvements are promising, but metabolic benefits remain inconsistent.

Nicotinamide Mononucleotide (NMN)

NMN is one step closer to NAD+ than NR â€” it's the direct precursor that NAMPT converts to NAD+. In theory, this should make it more efficient.

Key trials:

  • Imai et al. (2020): In healthy older men, 250mg/day NMN for 12 weeks was safe and increased NAD+ levels.

  • Yoshino et al. (2021): In postmenopausal women with prediabetes, 250mg/day NMN for 10 weeks improved muscle insulin sensitivity and signaling â€” a promising metabolic result.

  • Huang et al. (2022): In amateur runners, 600-1200mg/day NMN for 6 weeks improved aerobic capacity and endurance â€” suggesting benefits for physical performance.

  • Liao et al. (2022): In middle-aged adults, 600mg/day NMN for 60 days improved walking speed and general health scores.

Bottom line on NMN: Human trials are smaller and newer than NR, but results are promising for metabolic function and physical performance. The FDA ban in 2022 complicates access for U.S. consumers.

Niacin (Nicotinic Acid)

Plain niacin â€” the original vitamin B3 discovered in 1937 â€” also raises NAD+ levels. It's the cheapest option by far.

Key points:

  • Niacin reliably increases NAD+ but causes flushing (skin redness, warmth, itching) at higher doses.
  • Extended-release niacin reduces flushing but has been linked to liver toxicity at high doses.
  • The flushing response itself may have benefits â€” it dilates blood vessels and improves circulation.
  • Niacin has decades of safety data and costs pennies per day compared to -100/month for NR/NMN.
  • However, it requires higher doses (500-2000mg) to significantly raise NAD+, making side effects more likely.

The 2022 FDA NMN Ban

In November 2022, the FDA declared NMN an 'investigational new drug' (IND) because MetroBiotech had submitted it for pharmaceutical development. Under U.S. law, substances under IND status cannot be sold as dietary supplements.

This ruling effectively banned NMN supplement sales in the United States. Companies selling NMN faced FDA enforcement actions. However:

  • NR was NOT affected by this ruling.
  • NMN is still available in some other countries.
  • The ban is being legally challenged by supplement industry groups.
  • Some U.S. companies continue selling NMN under the legal theory that it was marketed as a supplement before the IND was filed.

4. Practical Application: Dosing, Timing, and Sources

NMN Protocol

  • Dose: 250-1000mg/day
  • Timing: Morning, on an empty stomach. NAD+ levels naturally peak in the morning, and some research suggests mimicking this circadian rhythm may enhance benefits.
  • Form: Capsules or powder. Sub-lingual forms claim better absorption but lack solid evidence.
  • Cost: -150/month (pre-ban pricing; current pricing is volatile due to regulatory uncertainty)
  • Availability: Limited in U.S. due to FDA ban; more available internationally

NR Protocol

  • Dose: 300-1000mg/day (most human trials used 500-1000mg)
  • Timing: Morning with food may reduce stomach upset, though fasting may enhance absorption.
  • Form: Capsules, typically as nicotinamide riboside chloride (NRCl)
  • Cost: -100/month
  • Availability: Widely available; not affected by FDA ban
  • Brands with third-party testing: Tru Niagen (Chromadex), Thorne, Life Extension

Niacin Protocol

  • Dose: 500-2000mg/day (start low and titrate up)
  • Timing: With food to reduce stomach upset; extended-release at bedtime may reduce flushing.
  • Form: Immediate-release (more flushing), extended-release (less flushing but potential liver risk at high doses), or flush-free (inositol hexanicotinate â€” but this may not raise NAD+ effectively)
  • Cost: -20/month
  • Availability: Over-the-counter at any pharmacy or grocery store

Combining with Other Interventions

Research suggests NAD+ boosters may work synergistically with:

  • Exercise: Physical activity naturally raises NAD+ through NAMPT activation. Combining exercise with supplementation may produce additive effects.
  • Fasting/Caloric restriction: These also raise NAD+ through sirtuin activation. NAD+ boosters may mimic some benefits of fasting without the hunger.
  • Resveratrol: Activates SIRT1 but requires NAD+ to function. Some researchers theorize resveratrol + NAD+ boosters may be synergistic, though human evidence is lacking.
  • TMG (trimethylglycine): NAD+ metabolism consumes methyl groups; some experts recommend taking TMG alongside NAD+ boosters to prevent methyl depletion, though this is theoretical.

5. Risks and Considerations

Known Side Effects

NMN: - Generally well-tolerated in human trials - Some users report mild stomach upset, flushing, or headaches - Long-term safety data is limited (most trials are 6-12 weeks)

NR: - Well-tolerated in most studies - Mild side effects: nausea, fatigue, headaches, stomach upset - At very high doses (2000mg+), some liver enzyme elevations have been reported

Niacin: - Flushing (red, warm, itchy skin) â€” harmless but uncomfortable - Stomach upset, nausea - Liver toxicity at high doses (especially extended-release) - Can worsen gout by increasing uric acid - May worsen peptic ulcers - Interferes with diabetes blood sugar control at high doses

Important Unknowns

  • Long-term safety: Most human trials last 6-12 weeks. We don't know the effects of years of supplementation.
  • Cancer risk: NAD+ fuels cell growth. In theory, raising NAD+ could accelerate existing cancers. No human evidence of this, but it's a theoretical concern that keeps researchers cautious.
  • Methyl depletion: NAD+ metabolism uses methyl groups. Long-term supplementation might deplete methyl donors, potentially affecting homocysteine levels and cardiovascular risk. Adding TMG or B-vitamins may mitigate this.
  • Sirtuin overactivation: More isn't always better. Excessive sirtuin activation might impair wound healing or immune responses in some contexts.
  • Individual variation: Response varies enormously between people based on baseline NAD+ levels, age, diet, and genetics.

Who Should Avoid or Use Caution

  • People with liver disease (especially with niacin)
  • People with gout (niacin raises uric acid)
  • People with active cancer or history of cancer (theoretical concern)
  • People taking chemotherapy (potential interactions)
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women (insufficient safety data)
  • People on immunosuppressants

6. Key Takeaways

  • NAD+ declines with age and this matters. It's not just a biomarker â€” it's a driver of cellular dysfunction. Restoring NAD+ levels is a logical longevity intervention, but the human evidence is still developing.

  • NR has the strongest human evidence. Multiple well-designed trials show it safely raises NAD+ and may improve blood pressure and arterial function. It's the safest bet for now, and it's legally available.

  • NMN shows promise but access is complicated. Early human trials suggest metabolic and performance benefits, but the FDA ban has made it difficult to obtain legally in the U.S. The regulatory situation may evolve.

  • Niacin works and is dirt cheap. It raises NAD+ reliably but causes flushing and requires higher doses. If you can tolerate the flush, it's the most cost-effective option. Extended-release reduces flushing but carries liver risks at high doses.

  • None of these are miracle drugs. Human benefits have been modest compared to the dramatic results in mice. NAD+ boosters may provide incremental benefits â€” better blood pressure, slightly improved metabolism, enhanced exercise capacity â€” but they're not turning back the clock 20 years.

  • Lifestyle matters more. Exercise, fasting, adequate sleep, and calorie moderation all raise NAD+ naturally and have far stronger evidence for longevity. Supplements should complement, not replace, these fundamentals.

  • Safety is reasonable but long-term data is lacking. Short-term trials show good tolerability, but we don't know the effects of 5-10 years of continuous use. The theoretical cancer concern remains unproven but warrants caution.

  • If you choose to supplement, NR is the most accessible evidence-based option. Start with 250-500mg/day, take it in the morning, and monitor your blood pressure and how you feel. Consider adding TMG if supplementing long-term. And remember â€” the goal is healthy aging, not just higher NAD+ numbers.
